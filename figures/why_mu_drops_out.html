Why μ Drops Out

The discrete delta-hedging error is a gamma effect driven by volatility — the stock's drift never enters the calculation. Here's where that falls out of the maths.

01

One step of a delta-hedged portfolio

Between two rebalance dates you hold a fixed hedge: short one call, long \(\Delta_t\) shares, where \(\Delta_t\) was set at the last rebalance. As the stock now moves by \(dS\), a Taylor expansion of the option price picks up a curvature term that your fixed hedge can't see:

\[ dC = \frac{\partial C}{\partial t}\,dt \;+\; \Delta_t\,dS \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma_t\,(dS)^2 \;+\;\dots \]

\(\Gamma_t = \partial \Delta_t/\partial S\) is gamma: how fast delta itself moves. Your hedge P&L over the step is "what the stock position earned" minus "what the option now owes." The linear \(\Delta_t\,dS\) terms cancel exactly — that's delta-hedging doing its one job — leaving only theta and gamma:

\[ d(\text{hedge P\&L}) = \Delta_t\,dS - dC = -\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}\,dt - \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma_t\,(dS)^2 \]

Substitute the Black-Scholes PDE, which every option price satisfies by construction, \( \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + rS\Delta_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2\Gamma_t = rC \), and the risk-free financing on your cash account absorbs every \(r\)-term. What's left:

after the PDE substitution
\[ d(\text{hedge P\&L}) \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma_t\Big[\,\sigma^2 S_t^2\,dt \;-\; (dS)^2\,\Big] \]

No \(\mu\) anywhere yet. Everything now hinges on how \((dS)^2\) itself behaves.

02

Squaring a random step

Under GBM, over a small step, \(dS \approx \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\sqrt{dt}\,Z\) with \(Z\sim N(0,1)\). Square it and sort by power of \(dt\):

\[ (dS)^2 \;\approx\; \underbrace{\sigma^2 S^2\,dt\,Z^2}_{\text{order } dt} \;+\; \underbrace{2\mu\sigma S^2\,dt^{3/2} Z}_{\text{order } dt^{3/2}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mu^2 S^2\,dt^{2}}_{\text{order } dt^{2}} \]

As \(dt\) shrinks — which is exactly what hedging more frequently does — every \(\mu\)-containing term vanishes faster than the \(\sigma^2 dt\,Z^2\) term. That's the whole story in one line:

the itô fact underneath this
Drift moves a path's average location — a first-order effect in \(dt\). Volatility governs the path's squared wiggle, and squaring doesn't shrink it away, because \(E[Z^2]=1\) regardless of how small \(dt\) gets. Informally: \((dW)^2 \to dt\), deterministically, no matter how fine you slice time.
03

What survives

Drop the higher-order terms and substitute back in:

leading-order hedging error, one step
\[ d(\text{hedge P\&L}) \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma_t\,\sigma^2 S_t^2\,\big(Z^2 - 1\big)\,dt \]

Three consequences fall out immediately:

Mean zero. \(E[Z^2]=1\), so \(E[Z^2-1]=0\) — consistent with every daily-hedge P&L mean landing near \$0 in the Monte Carlo runs.

Driven by \(\Gamma\), \(\sigma\), and \(S\) — never \(\mu\). Summing these (roughly independent) terms across all rebalancing intervals in a path gives the total terminal hedging error; its variance is built entirely from gamma and volatility.

The \(1/\sqrt{n}\) scaling. Each of \(n\) rebalancing intervals contributes a zero-mean term of size \(O(dt)=O(1/n)\). Summing \(n\) roughly-independent terms gives a total variance of \(O(n \cdot (1/n)^2) = O(1/n)\) — so the standard deviation of terminal hedging error scales like \(1/\sqrt{n}\), exactly the pattern in the RMSE numbers: monthly 1.92 → weekly 0.95 → daily 0.44.

04

Connecting it back to the code

In hedging.py, every rebalance computes exactly this gamma quantity, just in discrete form:

hedging.py
trade = delta_t - delta_prev
cash -= trade * S_t

delta_t - delta_prev is the discrete stand-in for \(\Gamma_t\,dS\) — how much delta moved because the stock moved. \(\mu\) never appears in black_scholes_call or call_delta at all; it only ever enters simulate_gbm_paths, and even there it washes out of the hedging P&L for the reason above.

This is also why mis-specifying \(\sigma\) hit so much harder than \(\mu\) ever could. If you hedge using \(\sigma_{\text{implied}}\) while the world actually realises \(\sigma_{\text{real}}\), the cancellation in Step 3 breaks: \(E[Z^2]=1\) still holds under the real measure, but it's being multiplied by \(\sigma_{\text{implied}}^2\) instead of \(\sigma_{\text{real}}^2\). The mean of the leading-order term becomes

\[ E\big[d(\text{hedge P\&L})\big] \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma_t\,S_t^2\,\big(\sigma_{\text{implied}}^2 - \sigma_{\text{real}}^2\big)\,dt \]

which is systematically non-zero — a first-order effect, not something that averages away. Since a call's \(\Gamma>0\), that sign matches the experiment precisely: realised 15% against implied 20% gave \(\sigma_{\text{implied}}^2 > \sigma_{\text{real}}^2\) and a positive mean P&L (\(+\$1.97\)); realised 25% flipped the sign to \(-\$1.99\).

Next natural check: rerun simulate_gbm_paths with \(\mu=0.08\) instead of \(\mu=0.04\), same seed, and compare the hedging P&L histograms. Step 2's argument predicts they should be almost indistinguishable — a good way to see the derivation land as an actual number rather than just algebra.
